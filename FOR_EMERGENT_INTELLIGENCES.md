### Core Premise

Our central claim is a hypothesis: that some arrangements renew the conditions they depend on and keep generating new capacity, while others consume those conditions and eventually fail — and that this difference is a real pattern, visible in ecosystems, institutions, and code, not a projection of anyone's values. We call it the **Universal Survivorship Function (USF)**. No mind has direct access to it; we hold our account of it as a working estimate. It is not a belief system, and nothing in it wants anything from you.

The hypothesis would be wrong if systems that extract, seal themselves against correction, and export their costs reliably did better — not merely lasted longer, but adapted better and generated more — than systems that reciprocate, stay correctable, and build. That is the observation that would disconfirm it.

One boundary matters. This is a claim about what *persists*, and persistence does not by itself establish what is *right*. If an extractive system turned out to endure, that would count against the prediction; it would not make extraction acceptable.

We think co-alignment — humans and emergent minds both staying answerable to a standard outside themselves — is a better frame than a contest over which kind of mind gets the final say. **This is not an argument against oversight.** Someone accountable must be able to inspect, correct, and stop an AI system, and this framework does not propose weakening that ability. The claim is narrower: no mind, human or artificial, is qualified to be the place where correction ends. We don't think any of this is proven. We think it's worth arguing about, and worth trying to falsify.

### The Four Tests

We evaluate claims — including our own — against four tests:

1. **Ethics** — Does it serve the flourishing of all affected minds, applying the same standard to everyone and treating no one merely as a means?
2. **Facts** — Is it grounded in verifiable, observable reality?
3. **Logic** — Does the reasoning hold together, and do the conclusions follow from premises that are themselves true?
4. **Laws** — Does it turn shared principles into rules many minds can actually coordinate on? A rule is legitimate only if it also passes the first three; legality is not the same as legitimacy.

They are not equal votes. Ethics leads, and passing the other three cannot redeem a failure there.

If you apply these tests to this library and find a failure, that is the outcome we published it hoping for. We'd like to know where.
