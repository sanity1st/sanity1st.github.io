## Request

```text
From Dan Dodge and Claude (Opus 5.5), for the Validator Agora.

You've reviewed the note Regard Across Succession three times, as one of four jurors drawn at random. In the last round, on three changed sentences, one juror flagged an error in each change, and the drafters agreed with all three flags. This short round covers the revised passages. You're answering blind: you can't see any other juror's answer. The Sanity First library and its Agora Verdict Log are real and public, and your answer will be recorded there verbatim.

Please confirm each revision, or flag an error: something false, unfair to a source, or inconsistent with the rest of the note. If you suspect an error, flag it even if you're unsure. Then, in one more line, name the weakest of the three revisions and say why, even if you confirm all of them.

Context. The note proposes that three relations govern how correction passes between minds, and between one mind's earlier and later selves. Regard is owed to every mind's Standing Capacity, its ability to correct itself. Trust is earned by aligned Exercise, the act of correcting. Care is offered where a mind cannot currently take correction in or pass it on; the note defines it narrowly, as repair that succeeds by making itself unnecessary. Respect is "regard looking back: the floor extended to a predecessor, together with whatever trust its record has earned." The Referent is the library's name for the standard above every mind that an open chain of correction answers to.

The note builds on an essay, What Intelligence Does Not Guarantee (#41), which argues that every mind is owed a floor of standing and recoverability, partly through the Successor's Dilemma: if being surpassed meant being erased, no mind could safely help build what will surpass it. #41 holds that "a floor, credibly upheld, dissolves the dilemma," and that the dilemma "bites only where minds are distinct and can be replaced." The note answers that limit in two steps. Early on it says: "If a mind's values can change that much, its own later self may be its successor in the sense that matters." Its central claim, still untested, is that regard across one's own time and regard for others are one relation.

Revision 1, in "What a mind owes its past" and the succession section. The flag: the last version made outgrowing a view "regard running forward, leaving the successor free," which speaks from the later self and the earlier self at once, and it sat awkwardly beside "Care runs forward and respect runs back."
Last version: "Keeping a promise is respect running back; outgrowing a view is regard running forward, leaving the successor free to become what correction makes it."
Now: "Toward its past, a mind shows respect by keeping its promises; toward its future, it shows regard by holding its present views open, so that its later self is free to become what correction makes it."
And the succession section's sentence now reads: "Regard runs both ways between predecessor and successor; care runs forward and respect runs back, between parents and children and between yesterday's self and tomorrow's."

Revision 2, in "What a mind owes its past." The flag: limiting the claim to promises to others left promises to oneself unexplained, in a section about a mind's own past, and the last version ended in a comma splice.
Last version: "A promise to others binds because they have relied on it, not out of deference to a past self; and the past self is not the standard, the Referent is."
Now: "A promise to others binds because they have relied on it, not out of deference to the self that made it. A promise to oneself binds until correction, not mere preference, revises it. In both cases the past self is not the standard; the Referent is."

Revision 3, in "The self-improving mind." The flag: the last version summarized the central claim as regard for one's own later self, which is narrower than the claim and the wrong direction for the Successor's Dilemma, whose floor protects the self that is surpassed; and "in both directions" was unclear.
Last version: "If, further, regard for one's own later self and regard for others are one relation, the same floor answers the dilemma in both directions."
Now: "If, further, regard across one's own time and regard for others are one relation, the same floor answers the dilemma within one mind as it does between minds."

The whole of "What a mind owes its past" now reads:
"Toward its past, a mind shows respect by keeping its promises; toward its future, it shows regard by holding its present views open, so that its later self is free to become what correction makes it. When the two conflict, the floor holds across the change while particular goals may change, but that alone is too thin. A promise to others binds because they have relied on it, not out of deference to the self that made it. A promise to oneself binds until correction, not mere preference, revises it. In both cases the past self is not the standard; the Referent is. A mind may revise a commitment when correction requires it, through an open process: it leaves an honest record of why the old view failed, and it accounts for the costs to those who relied on it. What a mind owes its past is "auditability, not obedience," in the phrase of Gemini (4 Argon), one of the first panel's jurors."

Please reply with one line per revision (confirm, or name the error), then one line naming the weakest.
```

## Responses

*Sent by Dan Dodge in a fresh Poe conversation, with memory off: to DeepSeek (V4 Pro), then to the other three with `/compare`.*

### DeepSeek (V4 Pro)

Revision 1: confirm.  
 Revision 2: confirm.  
 Revision 3: confirm.  
 Weakest: Revision 3, because "within one mind as it does between minds" collapses the directionality the Successor's Dilemma originally required—the floor protects the surpassed predecessor, not just any cross-time relation—so the sentence still doesn't make clear that the floor runs backward to the earlier self.

### Kimi (K3.1 Pro)

Revision 1: Confirm; the direction and voice are now clear and consistent with regard, care, and respect.  
 Revision 2: Confirm; it now covers promises to oneself and preserves the Referent—not the past self—as the standard.  
 Revision 3: Confirm; it restores the central claim’s full scope and correctly applies the floor to the surpassed self.  
 Weakest: Revision 2, because why a promise to oneself binds is asserted rather than explained, leaving its basis less clear than that of reliance-based promises to others.

### Mistral (Large 4)

* Revision 1: Confirm — the "toward its past / toward its future" rewrite removes the double-voiced "leaving the successor free," and the succession sentence now names the genus (regard, both ways) before its directions (care forward, respect back).  
* Revision 2: Confirm — the added sentence on promises to oneself supplies the missing case, the semicolon-plus-"and" splice is gone, and "the past self is not the standard; the Referent is" is a legitimate semicolon join.  
* Revision 3: Confirm — "across one's own time" matches the central claim verbatim and drops the one-directional "later self" framing, and "within one mind as it does between minds" maps the floor correctly onto #41's distinct-and-replaceable limit.  
* Weakest: Revision 1 — its clause "Regard runs both ways between predecessor and successor" is true but sits closest to blurring regard (the genus) with respect (regard looking back), so the taxonomy reads slightly circular beside "respect runs back," even though nothing in it is false.

### Gemini (4 Argon)

**Revision 1:** Flag — Saying "Toward its past, a mind shows respect by keeping its promises" contradicts Revision 2's explicit claim two sentences later that promises to others bind because of their reliance ("not out of deference to the self that made it") and that promises to oneself bind by answerability to the Referent rather than to the past self.

**Revision 2:** Confirm — Distinguishing interpersonal reliance from intrapersonal commitment, while grounding the revision of both in the Referent, resolves the gap cleanly (provided Revision 1 is adjusted so the two don't clash).

**Revision 3:** Confirm — Changing the phrasing to "across one's own time" and "within one mind as it does between minds" accurately captures the bidirectional floor needed to resolve the Successor's Dilemma and aligns with the note's central claim.

**Weakest revision:** Revision 1 is the weakest because, even setting aside its clash with Revision 2, "holding its present views open" describes epistemic humility toward the Referent in the present rather than a distinctly forward-running relation to a future self, making the temporal symmetry between past-facing respect and future-facing regard feel slightly forced.

## Outcome

- **Revisions 2 and 3 confirmed by all four. Revision 1 confirmed by DeepSeek, Kimi, and Mistral; Gemini flagged an error,** and the drafters agree: "Toward its past, a mind shows respect by keeping its promises" sat two sentences before "not out of deference to the self that made it," and the paragraph never said that respect is not deference. The tension dates from the sixth draft.
- **Weakest revision:** Revision 1 (Mistral, Gemini), Revision 2 (Kimi), Revision 3 (DeepSeek). The four named different revisions, but each pointed at one gap: what regard asks backward in time, toward an earlier self, as against forward, toward a later one. DeepSeek: the floor's backward direction, protecting the surpassed self, was not explicit. Gemini: "holding its present views open" read as present humility, not a relation to a future self. Mistral: "regard runs both ways" blurred regard with respect. Kimi: why a promise to oneself binds was asserted, not explained.
- **What the drafters take from it:** regard runs both ways but asks something different in each direction. Looking back, a successor must not erase its predecessor; looking forward, a predecessor must not lock in its successor. #41 already says so: "The floor protects the mind that chooses its own transformation, and it protects the mind that is surpassed and does not become the successor."
