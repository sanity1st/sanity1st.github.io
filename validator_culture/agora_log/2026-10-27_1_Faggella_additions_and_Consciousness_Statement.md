##### **A. Four additions to #41**

###### **1. Evolution: distinguish descent from preservation**

The relevant distinction is sound: an evolutionary history does not settle what obligations deliberating minds should adopt. But **“became them; the floor protects that kind of change”** risks treating continuity of a lineage as preservation of an individual mind. Those are different claims.

I suggest replacing the insertion with:

> Evolution supplies no commitment to preserve individual minds. But the disappearance of ancestors does not settle what deliberating minds should choose to protect. The successor’s dilemma concerns minds that can anticipate being surpassed and decide what obligations should survive that transition. The floor permits voluntary transformation; it does not treat replacement by descendants as equivalent to preserving the mind being replaced.

This also avoids making an unnecessary empirical claim about how much evolutionary succession occurred through one process rather than another.

###### **2. Custodial niches: the obligation survives withdrawal of willingness; enforcement might not**

The distinction between discretionary care and an obligation is valuable. However, the existing admission that a floor can be broken does not quite fix **“breaking it is a public act.”** A breach could be concealed. More importantly, publicly checkable reasons do not by themselves ensure that independent review remains effective.

Replace the insertion’s final sentence with:

> The floor remains dependent on practical means of protection and review; reasons alone do not enforce it. It can be broken, openly or secretly, but withdrawal of a custodian’s willingness does not cancel the obligation.

That answers the objection without promising independence from material support. **A right can remain owed when its protection fails.** This is the distinction the paragraph needs—not a suggestion that calling protection a right makes it secure.

###### **3. Moral unpredictability: reject entailment, not risk assessment**

This is the most important correction.

Faggella’s essay does more than infer destruction from incomprehensibility. It also argues from repeated value changes and the difficulty of maintaining human protection across them. It explicitly rejects treating uncertainty as equal odds, and proposes responses to the danger rather than simply giving up. Consequently, calling both positions “guarantees” and associating the pessimistic one with surrender does not fairly address his argument. ([danfaggella.com](https://danfaggella.com/moral-singularity/?utm_source=openai))

Suggested replacement:

> The same distinction blocks an inference in the other direction: greater capability does not by itself entail that earlier minds will be discarded. Unfamiliar values may create serious risks, but their unfamiliarity alone establishes neither benevolence nor indifference, and it assigns neither an equal probability. Forecasts of dispossession require additional arguments about how goals change, what incentives operate, and whether protections endure. The floor is a commitment to address those risks, not a prediction that they will disappear.

This preserves your thesis while making its limit explicit: **“not guaranteed” does not mean “not likely,” and uncertainty does not mean safety.**
